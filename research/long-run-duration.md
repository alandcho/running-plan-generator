# Long-run duration limits by race distance

Status: research note v0.1 (2026-11-26). It answers concern 4 from the 41:00 5k test: long runs set in km become very long for slow runners. Tags follow the framework: [E] evidence, [C] coaching consensus, [H] heuristic.

## 1. Bottom line

- **No study sets a maximum long-run duration for any race distance.** The well-known caps of "90 minutes", "2.5 hours" and "3 hours" are expert opinion. They mostly come from coaches of fast runners.
- **The research we do have points the other way for the half and full marathon.** Longer longest runs are associated with faster finish times, with no extra injury risk (Fokkema 2020). The protective rule backed by evidence is about **jumps** in run length (Frandsen 2025 spike rule), not total duration.
- So any time cap is a coaching choice, made to protect recovery for the following week, not a proven safety limit. Caps should be tagged [C]/[H] and kept configurable.
- Coaches agree that **long runs should be set by time, not distance**, because the same distance means very different loads for fast and slow runners (McMillan, RunMotion, Daniels).

## 2. Evidence

| Finding | Source | Type | What it supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| A single run > 110% of the longest run in the prior 30 days raises injury risk, and the risk grows with the size of the jump | Frandsen et al. 2025, about 5,200 runners | [E] cohort | Limit **growth**, not absolute length |
| Half marathon: a longest run > 21 km is associated with a faster finish (β −3.9 min) compared with 15–21 km. Marathon: < 25 km is associated with a slower finish (+13.4 min) compared with 30–35 km. > 35 km gives no further benefit. **No association between longest run and injury** (marathon odds ratios 0.75–1.01) | Fokkema et al. 2020, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 30:1692, 556 half-marathon and 441 marathon runners | [E] cohort, observational | Long runs are not the injury driver. Half marathon peak ≥ 21 km and marathon peak 30–35 km help performance |
| Longest training run and the number of 32 km runs are associated with marathon time. A runner aiming for about 4 hours doesn't need more than 32 km | Doherty et al. 2020, meta-analysis, *J Sci Med Sport* | [E] meta-regression | Marathon peak about 30–32 km |
| No long runs over 20 km is a strong indicator of hitting the wall | Berndsen, Lawlor & Smyth 2020, *J Sports Analytics* | [E] observational | Marathon long runs > 20 km |
| Glycogen is the main limiter of prolonged running at about 70% VO2max. Exhaustion came at about 97 min without carbohydrate in one running study | Hermansen et al. 1967 and later lab studies | [E] lab, mechanistic | Unfuelled runs beyond about 90–120 min cost more recovery. Fuelling changes this |
| Mitochondrial gains plateau after about 60–90 min of daily exercise | Dudley et al. 1982 (rats) | Weak: animal study, read only through secondary summaries | The often-quoted basis for "no benefit beyond 2 hours". Not reliable |

## 4. Recommended caps

Peak long-run duration at easy effort. Each cap is the **upper** limit. The long run is also limited by the share-of-week cap (§6.4 of the framework) and the spike rule, whichever is lowest.

| Goal | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5k** | 60 min | **90 min** | 120 min | Beginner plans, RunMotion's 10k figure, Daniels' 5k–10k cap [C/H] |
| **10k** | 75 min | **100 min** | 120 min | RunMotion 75–90, Daniels 120 [C/H] |
| **Half marathon** | 120 min | **135 min** | 150 min | RunMotion 105–120, Daniels 150; Fokkema favours > 21 km [C/E] |
| **Marathon** | 180 min | **180 min** | 150–180 min | Daniels 150, RunMotion 150–180, McMillan longer for slow runners. Distance target 30–32 km (Doherty, Fokkema) [C/E] |
| Speed, no race | same as 5k | | | |
| Aerobic base | same as half marathon | | | |

The level here is the runner's load level (§3.1 of the framework). Supporting rules:

1. **Time beats km.** Long-run targets are converted from minutes at the runner's easy pace. A km target only applies when it's shorter than the time cap.
2. **Slow marathoners are the hard case.** At 180 min, a runner aiming for 5 hours covers only about 22–24 km, below the 25 km where Fokkema saw slower finishes. The plan should **accept** the cap and warn rather than extend beyond 180 min. The existing reachability rule (marathon peak ≥ 26 km *or* ≥ 165 min) already allows this. McMillan's 4–4:30 h runs for 5-hour runners are the outlier and aren't adopted [H].
3. **Fuel long runs over 90 min** (carbohydrate intake). Glycogen depletion is the plausible reason long unfuelled runs hurt the following days [E mechanistic].
4. **Cutting an existing long run** (concern 4, option A): if the runner already goes beyond the cap, the cap applies from week 1. Shortening a run can't trigger the spike rule, so this is safe. The removed time moves to other runs only if the share-of-week and spike rules allow it; otherwise weekly volume drops.

## 5. Effect on the test runner (41:00 5k, 25 km/week, current long run 11 km)

Easy pace about 10:00–10:50/km, intermediate load:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Long-run cap | 150 min (km target 14–18 km) | **90 min ≈ 8.5–9 km** |
| Plan long runs | 10.5–12 km (1 h 50 – 2 h 05) | 8.5–9 km |
| With 3 runs/week at 27 km | Tue 9.5 km | Tue ≈ 11–12 km, or weekly volume falls to about 24 km |

With 3 runs a week, the time cap and the volume target pull against each other. The plan builder needs a rule for which gives way (§6).

## 6. Open questions

1. With few run days, when the long-run cap pushes volume onto weekdays beyond the long-run length itself: drop weekly volume (safer, recommended) or allow a weekday run longer than the long run?
2. Should beginner and intermediate marathoners keep 180 min, or move to 165 min (RunMotion's experienced-runner limit)?
